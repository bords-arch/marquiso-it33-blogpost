Seeing Our Risk Assessment Through an Auditor's Eyes

When my group completed our Risk Assessment Form, we were proud of it. We identified threats, rated likelihood and impact, and recommended treatments that made sense. But once I put on an auditor's hat, the question changed. An assessor doesn't ask, "Did you think about risk?" An auditor asks, "Can you prove it?" That shift exposed gaps in our work and in the organization we assessed, and it changed how I think about security documentation.

Starting with the question auditors always ask first

Nearly every audit framework begins with some version of the same question: what do you own? Risk can't be assessed for assets nobody has identified. NIST's guidance on risk assessment treats the identification of assets and their value to the mission as a foundation for everything that follows, because threat, vulnerability, and impact judgments only mean something relative to what is being protected (Joint Task Force Transformation Initiative, 2012). ISO/IEC 27001:2022 makes the same point as a requirement, expecting an inventory of information and other associated assets with assigned owners (International Organization for Standardization [ISO], 2022).

Looking back at our form, the organization had a reasonable informal sense of what mattered. Staff could tell us which systems were critical, where sensitive records lived, and which services the business couldn't operate without. But "people know" is not an inventory. An auditor can't test someone's memory. They need a record that exists independently of any one person, is kept current, and can be compared against reality.

Which assets mattered most

Three categories of assets carried most of the risk in our assessment.

Information assets came first: records about the organization's clients and operations, plus the credentials and configuration data that guard access to everything else. Information is usually the real target, and it often sits scattered across systems, shared drives, and personal devices, which makes it the hardest asset to inventory.

Core systems and infrastructure came second: the servers, applications, network components, and cloud services that store, process, and move that information. Their importance comes from dependency. When one fails, many business functions go with it.

Supporting assets, including the people with privileged access, key third-party providers, and the physical spaces housing equipment, came third. These are the easiest to overlook, and an auditor will rarely let them slide. Over-reliance on a single administrator or a single vendor is a risk in its own right, even if it never appears on a hardware list.

Classification and ownership

Identifying assets is only the first step. Each one needs a classification and an owner, and these two ideas work together.

Classification tells the organization how much protection an asset deserves. A simple scheme works well: public, internal, confidential, and restricted, each tied to handling rules for storage, sharing, retention, and disposal. ISO/IEC 27001 expects information to be classified according to its legal requirements, value, criticality, and sensitivity (ISO, 2022). Classification also drives prioritization. A "restricted" asset with a weak control should jump the queue ahead of a "public" asset with the same weakness.

Ownership answers the accountability question. An asset owner is the person, ideally a role rather than a named individual, who decides how the asset is classified, who may access it, and whether its controls are adequate. This is different from a custodian, such as IT staff who run the system day to day. Many organizations blur the two, so the technical team ends up making business decisions about data it doesn't actually own. An auditor looking at ownership will ask whether each critical asset has a named accountable role, whether that role knows it holds the responsibility, and whether it has ever actually exercised it, for example by reviewing access or approving a change.

In our assessment, responsibility was understood in practice but rarely written down. Ownership lived in habit rather than in documentation. That works until someone leaves, a project changes hands, or an incident forces the question of who had the authority to decide.

What evidence an audit would require

This is where an auditor's view differs most from a risk assessor's. A risk assessment says a control should exist. An audit asks for evidence that it exists and works. NIST's assessment guidance describes three basic methods: examining documentation and records, interviewing personnel, and testing the mechanisms themselves (Joint Task Force, 2022). A strong audit uses all three, because a policy on paper, a confident answer in an interview, and a working technical control are different forms of proof.

For the controls our group recommended, an auditor would reasonably expect:

  • A current asset inventory with classification, owner, location, and lifecycle status, plus evidence that it is reviewed on a schedule.
  • Approved policies and procedures that match what staff actually do, with version history and approval records.
  • Access control records, including how access is granted, reviewed, and removed, and logs showing the process was followed for real people on real dates.
  • Change and patch records showing that updates are tested, approved, and applied within a defined timeframe.
  • Backup and recovery proof, meaning not just that backups run but that a restoration was tested and the results recorded.
  • Monitoring and incident records demonstrating that alerts are reviewed and that incidents, including near misses, are logged and followed up.
  • Training and awareness records showing who was trained, when, and on what.
  • Third-party assurance, such as contracts, security requirements, and reviews of critical providers.

The key phrase is "evidence of operation over time." A control that was configured once and never checked is a claim, not an assurance.

The gaps an auditor would likely find

Reflecting honestly, I think an auditor would find a familiar pattern, one that shows up in many organizations that are otherwise doing reasonable work.

First, the inventory would be incomplete or informal. Core systems would likely be accounted for, but data stores, cloud services adopted without formal review, and personal or temporary devices would not. An auditor reconciling the list against what is actually on the network typically finds assets nobody listed.

Second, ownership would be implied rather than assigned. Responsibility would be understood in conversation but not recorded, which makes it impossible to prove who was accountable for a decision.

Third, classification would be inconsistent or absent, meaning protection levels would be applied by instinct rather than by rule.

Fourth, controls would exist without a trail. Many protections would probably be working, but the logs, review sign-offs, and test results that prove it would be scattered, undated, or never produced. In audit terms, a control without evidence is treated as not operating.

Fifth, reviews would be reactive. Access rights, policies, and risk ratings tend to get revisited after a problem rather than on a planned cycle.

None of these gaps means the organization was careless. They reflect the normal distance between doing security work and demonstrating it. But that distance is exactly what an audit measures.

How an inventory and checklist strengthen our treatments

Our group's recommended treatments were sensible: tighter access controls, improved backup practices, better monitoring, and clearer procedures. The auditor's lens showed me that each of them depends on two supporting tools.

An asset inventory makes treatments targetable. We can't apply stronger access controls to a system we haven't listed, or verify backups for data we haven't classified. NIST's control catalog treats inventory as a foundational control family for this reason, since many other controls rely on knowing which components exist and who is responsible for them (Joint Task Force, 2020). The inventory also turns risk ratings into something stable. Instead of rating risk in the abstract each time, we can tie each risk to a specific asset, owner, and classification, which makes priorities easier to defend.

An audit checklist makes treatments verifiable. For each recommended control, the checklist would state the control objective, the evidence required, where that evidence lives, who provides it, and how often it is reviewed. That turns "we recommend better monitoring" into "monitoring alerts are reviewed weekly, and the review log is retained for twelve months." The first is an intention; the second can be tested. A checklist also creates a natural improvement loop. Each audit cycle shows which controls produced clean evidence and which didn't, and those results feed back into the next risk assessment.

Together, the two tools close the loop that a risk assessment alone leaves open: identify, assign, protect, verify, and review.

Final reflection

Revisiting our form as an auditor made me value documentation differently. I used to think of records as paperwork that follows the real security work. Now I see them as part of the control itself, because an organization that can't demonstrate its protections can't reliably maintain them, defend them to a regulator, or learn from their failures. If I could revise our assessment, I would add an inventory reference, an owner, and an evidence requirement to every recommended treatment. The risk would be no smaller, but the organization would finally be able to show that it is managing it.
